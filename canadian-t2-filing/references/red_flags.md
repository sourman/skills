# Red Flags — Investigate Before Proceeding

When you encounter any of these patterns during categorization, STOP and ask the user before classifying. Each flag includes what to ask.

---

## 1. Recurring Payments to an Individual

**Pattern:** Monthly or regular payments to the same person, similar amounts, no deliverables or invoices tied to the payments.

**Could be:** Shareholder buyout installments, not contractor fees.

**Ask the user:**
- "What was [Name]'s role? Were they ever a shareholder or co-founder?"
- "Are these payments for services rendered, or related to a share purchase agreement?"
- "Did [Name] contribute capital to the business? Was it repaid?"

**Red flags in the data:**
- Payment notes containing "equity", "shares", "buyout", "installment", "agreement", "eqty"
- The user describes the person as "partner" or "co-founder" in passing
- Payments started around the time the person's name disappears from corporate records
- Large irregular lump sums mixed with regular smaller payments

**Tax impact:** Share purchases are non-deductible capital transactions. Contractor fees are deductible operating expenses. Misclassifying $15K+ creates audit risk.

---

## 2. Personal Pass-Throughs

**Pattern:** Money flows from business account → owner's personal account → somewhere else. Looks like a shareholder drawdown.

**Could be:** The owner routing money through a personal account to pay a corporate credit card (neutral, not a drawdown).

**Ask the user:**
- "When you transferred $X from the business to your personal account on [date], where did it go next?"
- "Was this to pay a corporate credit card, or did you keep the funds personally?"

**Red flags in the data:**
- An outgoing business transfer followed within 1-3 days by a credit card payment of approximately the same amount
- The credit card being paid is a corporate/business card
- The transfer amount closely matches the card payment (small difference = fees)

**Tax impact:** If it's a pass-through to pay a corporate card, it's neutral. If it's a genuine drawdown, it's a shareholder loan that must be repaid within 1 year of year-end or it becomes taxable income.

---

## 3. Zero-Revenue Years

**Pattern:** A tax year with $0 revenue, especially when surrounding years have revenue.

**Could be:** Revenue hiding in an unexpected account (personal PayPal, Venmo, bank account, freelance platform with a personal login).

**Ask the user:**
- "Are you sure there was no revenue in [year]?"
- "Did you receive any payments for work in a personal account — PayPal, Venmo, bank, freelance platform — that year?"
- "Were there any small jobs or gigs that might have been paid to you personally?"

**Tax impact:** Missing revenue means incorrect loss calculations, wrong balance sheets, and potentially wrong filing status.

---

## 4. Foreign Merchant HST Claims

**Pattern:** Claiming HST/GST ITC on purchases from foreign merchants or platforms.

**Could be:** The merchant didn't actually charge Canadian HST, so no ITC is available.

**Ask the user:**
- "Were your contractors/freelancers on Canadian or non-Canadian accounts on that platform?"
- "Does this foreign merchant actually charge Canadian HST? Some do (Airbnb for Canadian properties), most don't."

**Red flags in the data:**
- Merchant is clearly foreign (foreign currency, foreign address)
- Transactions routed through an agency or intermediary
- Platform charges HST on Canadian accounts but not on non-Canadian accounts
- Transactions in foreign currency where no Canadian tax was collected

**Tax impact:** HST depends on the *seller's* GST/HST registration, not the buyer's location. A Canadian business paying through a foreign intermediary may not have Canadian HST embedded. Overclaiming ITC creates liability.

---

## 5. Pre-Incorporation Expenses

**Pattern:** Business expenses dated before the incorporation date.

**Could be:** Legitimate pre-incorporation expenses claimable under specific tax provisions, or personal expenses that happened before the business existed.

**Ask the user:**
- "Was this expense related to setting up the business, or was it purely personal?"
- "Can you connect this purchase to a specific business purpose that existed before incorporation?"

**Tax impact:** Pre-incorporation business expenses are claimable (e.g., ITA s.69(1)(b) in Canada), but they must be genuine business expenses, not personal costs retroactively claimed. Document the business purpose.

---

## 6. Refunds and Reversals

**Pattern:** A refund or credit appearing after a previously categorized expense.

**Could be:** The expense was refunded, so both the original expense and the refund need to be netted against the same category. The ITC on the original expense may also need to be reversed.

**Ask the user:**
- "Is this refund related to a previous expense? Which one?"

**Tax impact:** Refunds reduce expense totals. If ITC was claimed on the original expense, the ITC must be reversed proportionally. Netting against the wrong category distorts the income statement.

---

## 7. Personal Expenses on Business Cards

**Pattern:** Transactions on corporate/business credit cards that look personal (groceries, personal subscriptions, personal travel).

**Could be:** A personal expense accidentally put on the business card, or a legitimate business expense that looks personal.

**Ask the user:**
- "Is this a business expense or personal? It appeared on your corporate card."

**Tax impact:** Personal expenses on corporate cards should be excluded or classified as shareholder loans. Including them inflates deductions. The inclusion philosophy says "include when plausible" — but personal groceries on a corporate card are not plausible.

---

## 8. Stolen, Lost, or Damaged Assets

**Pattern:** Capital assets (laptops, equipment) that were stolen, lost, or destroyed.

**Could be:** Terminal loss claimable from the CCA class, reducing the UCC balance.

**Ask the user:**
- "Was the asset insured? Were any proceeds received?"
- "What was the fair market value at the time of loss?"

**Tax impact:** A stolen/destroyed asset qualifies as a terminal loss. But in loss years, claiming the loss is wasted — it only increases an unused loss carryforward. Defer to profitable years.
